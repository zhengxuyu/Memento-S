# Genes as Words or Subroutines

Genes are like words in a language or subroutines in a program: they're modular units that can be reused in different contexts, combined in novel ways, and whose meaning depends partly on context. A gene doesn't have a fixed effect — its expression depends on other genes, environment, and developmental timing.

## When to Apply
- Explaining why genetic determinism is wrong (genes aren't blueprints)
- Analyzing how modularity and reuse work in biological or computational systems
- Drawing analogies between genetic and linguistic/computational information

## Procedure
1. Identify the gene (or module) and its apparent function
2. Check: does it always produce the same effect, or is it context-dependent?
3. Identify other contexts where the same gene/module is reused with different effects
4. Map the analogy: gene = word/subroutine, genome = sentence/program, expression = meaning/output
5. Note where the analogy breaks down (genes aren't perfectly modular)

## Example
The PAX6 gene is involved in eye development across wildly different species — flies, mice, humans. Same "subroutine," different organisms, different eye architectures. It's reused like a function call, but the output depends on the calling context (other genes, developmental environment).

## Anti-Pattern
Don't push the analogy to the point of claiming genes are literally code. Biological information is messier — genes overlap, read in multiple frames, and have context-sensitivity that exceeds most programming languages.
