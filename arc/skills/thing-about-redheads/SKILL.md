# A Thing About Redheads

"Redheads have fiery tempers" — even if statistically false, it illustrates how we use generalizations. The key insight: a generalization like "birds fly" is useful and largely true even though penguins and ostriches exist. Demanding exceptionless universals or dismissing all generalizations are both errors.

## When to Apply
- Someone rejects a useful generalization by citing a single counterexample
- A pattern holds "mostly" and you need to reason about its scope
- Distinguishing statistical truths from universal laws

## Procedure
1. State the generalization clearly
2. Identify its scope — is it meant as universal, statistical, or prototypical?
3. Check whether counterexamples refute it or are expected exceptions
4. Assess whether the generalization does useful predictive or explanatory work
5. Qualify it appropriately rather than either absolutizing or discarding it

## Example
"Mammals don't lay eggs" is a useful generalization even though platypuses exist. Dismissing it because of platypuses is silly. Treating it as exceptionless is also wrong. The right move: use it as a default expectation and note the exceptions.

## Anti-Pattern
Don't defend a generalization by ignoring counterexamples. Acknowledge them, assess their frequency, and adjust your confidence. Generalizations are tools, not dogma.
