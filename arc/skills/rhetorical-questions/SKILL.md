# Rhetorical Questions

A rhetorical question is an assertion wearing a question's clothing. "Isn't it obvious that X?" really means "X is true" but avoids the burden of proof. By converting a claim into a question, the speaker skips justification and makes disagreement feel socially awkward rather than intellectually necessary.

## When to Apply
- Reasoning contains questions that aren't genuinely seeking answers
- An argument's persuasive force comes from how a question is phrased
- You catch yourself asking "Wouldn't it make sense to..." instead of stating a testable hypothesis
- Evaluating whether an "obvious" question actually has a non-obvious answer

## Procedure
1. Identify questions in the reasoning that aren't genuine inquiries
2. Convert each rhetorical question into the assertion it implies
3. Evaluate that assertion on its merits — does evidence support it?
4. If unsupported, replace it with a genuine hypothesis to test

## Example
Reasoning: "Doesn't the player need to collect all items before the door opens?" Convert to assertion: "The player needs to collect all items before the door opens." Now evaluate: has this been tested? Have you seen the door open with some items uncollected? The question assumed the answer; the assertion demands evidence.

## Anti-Pattern
Not all questions in reasoning are rhetorical. Genuine inquiry ("What happens if I try X?") is valuable. Only flag questions that smuggle in untested conclusions.
