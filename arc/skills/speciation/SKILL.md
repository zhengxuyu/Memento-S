# When Does Speciation Occur?

There's no precise moment when one species becomes two — it's a gradual process. Yet "species" is an enormously useful concept. The boundary is drawn by convention, not discovered in nature. Many important distinctions share this feature: real and useful, yet without a sharp line.

## When to Apply
- Arguing about exactly when a transition occurred in a continuous process
- Debating precise thresholds or cutoffs
- A gradual change resists clean categorization

## Procedure
1. Identify the continuous process underlying the categorical distinction
2. Accept that no sharp boundary exists in the process itself
3. Ask: what practical purpose does drawing a boundary serve here?
4. Draw the boundary where it's most useful for your purposes
5. Don't mistake the boundary's arbitrariness for the distinction being meaningless

## Example
You're deciding when an agent has "learned" a game mechanic. There's no sharp moment — understanding accrues gradually. Set a practical threshold (e.g., 3 consecutive correct uses) rather than searching for the "real" moment of learning.

## Anti-Pattern
Don't use the lack of a sharp boundary to argue the distinction doesn't exist. "There's no exact moment of speciation" doesn't mean species aren't real.
