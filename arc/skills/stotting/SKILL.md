# How to Explain Stotting

Gazelles "stot" (jump high in place) when predators approach. This seems wasteful, but it's an honest signal: "I'm so fit I can waste energy." Predators learn to chase non-stotters. The signal works because it's costly to fake — a weak gazelle can't afford to stot.

## When to Apply
- Evaluating whether a signal or claim is trustworthy
- Designing mechanisms where honesty matters
- Assessing why seemingly wasteful behaviors persist

## Procedure
1. Identify the signal being sent
2. Ask: is this signal costly to produce?
3. Determine if a "faker" could afford to send the same signal
4. If faking is cheap, the signal is unreliable; if costly, it's more trustworthy
5. Look for the equilibrium: who benefits from honesty, who from deception?

## Example
An agent expends many actions thoroughly exploring a dead-end before reporting "no path here." This costly exploration is a more reliable signal than a quick glance — the agent "paid" for the information, making its conclusion trustworthy.

## Anti-Pattern
Don't assume all costly behaviors are signals. Sometimes waste is just waste, not an honest advertisement of quality.
