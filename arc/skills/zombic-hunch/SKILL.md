# The Zombic Hunch

Many people have a strong intuition that philosophical zombies (beings physically identical to us but lacking consciousness) are conceivable. Dennett calls this the "zombic hunch" and argues it's unreliable — we can't actually conceive of a full physical duplicate in enough detail to know consciousness is absent. The hunch outruns our actual ability to conceive.

## When to Apply
- Someone argues "I can conceive of X, therefore X is possible"
- An intuition about possibility seems strong but resists detailed specification
- Evaluating whether a thought experiment's premises are genuinely coherent

## Procedure
1. Identify the claim: "X is conceivable, therefore X is possible"
2. Ask: can you actually fill in the details of X, or just gesture at it?
3. Test: as you add detail, does the conceivability survive?
4. If the intuition fades under scrutiny, it was a hunch, not genuine conceivability
5. Don't grant conceivability arguments more weight than the detail supports

## Example
"I can conceive of an agent that behaves identically but has no internal states." Can you really? Specify the architecture fully — where exactly does the "inner state" disappear while behavior stays identical? The more detail you add, the harder this gets.

## Anti-Pattern
Don't dismiss all conceivability arguments. Some things genuinely are conceivable and that matters. The point is to demand rigor, not blanket skepticism.
